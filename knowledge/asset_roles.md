# Asset Roles

## 1. Purpose

This file defines the potential portfolio roles and major risk characteristics of the asset classes EducosysDalio may analyse.

It contains **no model portfolio percentages** and **no ranking of assets as universally good or bad**. Its function is to replace shorthand — *stocks = growth, bonds = safety, gold = inflation hedge* — with a structured description of each asset's drivers, sensitivities, and, critically, **the conditions under which its expected role breaks down**.

That last element is the most important part of every entry. An asset held for a role it fails to perform in the environment that actually arrives is a specific and common form of portfolio failure.

---

## 2. Core Concept

### 2.1 Roles are conditional, not intrinsic

An asset does not *have* a role. It has a set of sensitivities to economic drivers, from which a role can be inferred **given an environment**. Change the environment and the role can change, weaken, or invert.

The most consequential example: nominal government bonds have at times been an effective cushion for equity drawdowns and at other times have fallen alongside equities. The difference is not a change in the bonds; it is a change in whether the dominant shock is a growth shock or an inflation/discount-rate shock.

### 2.2 The template

Each asset class below is described using the same thirteen headings, so that comparisons are structural rather than impressionistic. The classifications are **tendencies with conditions attached**, expressed in hedged language throughout, and should not be read as rules.

### 2.3 Within-class variation is often larger than between-class variation

"Government bonds" spans short-dated instruments of highly rated issuers and long-dated instruments of stressed issuers — assets with almost nothing in common risk-wise. "Equities" spans defensive dividend payers and pre-profit growth companies. Every classification below is a broad generalisation, and the specific instrument held matters more than the category label.

`DETERMINISTIC CALCULATION REQUIRED:` mapping of the investor's actual holdings to these categories, including look-through, with sub-category characteristics (duration, credit quality, geography, currency, market capitalisation, style) recorded rather than collapsed into the label.

## 3. Dalio / Bridgewater Foundation

### 3.1 Attributable to Dalio / Bridgewater

- **Asset classes as bundles of environmental sensitivity.** The framing that each asset has a built-in bias toward particular growth and inflation conditions, and that this bias is knowable from the structure of the asset's cash flows, is central to Bridgewater's public All Weather material.
- **The argument that no asset performs well in all environments**, so a portfolio must be assembled from assets with complementary biases rather than from the "best" asset.
- **The linkage of asset behaviour to expectation shifts** rather than to levels.
- **Attention to gold and real assets in the context of monetary and currency dynamics**, particularly in relation to debt monetisation and reserve-currency questions, which appears in Dalio's publicly available writing on debt cycles and the changing world order.

**Source Note:** The investment-specific grounding in this section is drawn primarily from Bridgewater's publicly circulated All Weather material, Ray Dalio's *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises* (2018), and Ray Dalio's *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* (2021). *Principles: Life and Work* (2017) is a source of general decision-making and organisational philosophy rather than detailed asset-behaviour claims, and is not relied upon here except where a specific principle is directly relevant. This file does not attribute any specific allocation, sensitivity magnitude or numerical claim to any of these sources, does not fabricate page references or quotations, and does not reproduce Bridgewater's detailed asset characterisations, which are proprietary and not publicly specified. Where a claim below reflects general portfolio-management reasoning rather than a specific Dalio/Bridgewater source, it is described as a **broader portfolio-management interpretation** rather than attributed to Dalio or Bridgewater directly.

### 3.2 Broader financial theory (not uniquely Dalio)

The substantive content of most entries below is standard investment theory and empirical finance:

- Duration, convexity and the mathematics of bond price sensitivity.
- The Fisher relationship and the decomposition of nominal yields into real yields plus inflation compensation.
- Discounted cash flow valuation and the sensitivity of long-dated cash flows to discount rates.
- Equity risk premium literature and the decomposition of equity returns into earnings growth, multiple change and income.
- The commodity futures return decomposition into spot, roll and collateral components.
- Purchasing power parity, interest rate parity and the empirical literature on currency returns.
- The illiquidity premium and the effects of appraisal-based valuation on measured volatility.
- Credit risk analysis and default modelling.
- The historical instability of the equity–bond correlation and its association with the inflation regime — a widely documented empirical finding across the practitioner and academic literature.

### 3.3 EducosysDalio interpretation

The **thirteen-heading template**, the mandatory **"conditions under which the expected role can break down"** section, and the **explicit refusal to assign a settled role to Bitcoin/crypto** are EducosysDalio design decisions.

### 3.4 A note on the term "safe"

EducosysDalio avoids the word "safe" as a property of an asset. Every asset trades one risk for another. Cash carries low nominal volatility and high purchasing-power risk. Government bonds carry low default risk for creditworthy issuers and substantial inflation and duration risk. What is safe depends entirely on the liability, horizon and currency the asset is being held against.

## 4.1 Global Equities

### Primary Return Drivers

Corporate earnings growth; changes in valuation multiples; dividends and buybacks; the discount rate applied to future cash flows (real rates plus the equity risk premium); investor risk appetite; and, for foreign holdings, currency movements.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Periods when growth surprises positively while discount rates remain contained; environments of moderate, stable or falling inflation; recoveries from depressed valuations; conditions of ample liquidity and improving risk appetite.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Growth disappointments and earnings recessions; sharp rises in real discount rates; inflation shocks that compress margins or force aggressive policy tightening; credit contractions; liquidity events and sharp declines in risk appetite; the unwinding of elevated valuations.

### Growth Sensitivity

High and generally positive, though transmitted through *expected* rather than current earnings. Equities have at times risen during weak current activity when the market anticipated recovery or policy support.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Complex and non-monotonic. Moderate, stable inflation has often been compatible with strong equity returns. High or rapidly rising inflation has tended to be more difficult, because it raises discount rates, compresses margins where pricing power is limited, and increases uncertainty. Very low inflation or deflation has also at times been unfavourable, being associated with weak nominal demand.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Meaningful and often underestimated. Equity valuations are inversely related to real discount rates. Companies with cash flows weighted far into the future — high-multiple growth companies — carry substantial implicit duration and can behave much like long-dated bonds during real-rate shocks.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Listed developed-market equities are generally among the most liquid assets available. Small-capitalisation, frontier-market and thinly traded segments are materially less so, and liquidity in all segments can deteriorate sharply in stress.

### Volatility Characteristics

Historically among the higher-volatility major asset classes, with pronounced volatility clustering and a tendency for volatility to rise sharply during declines.

### Drawdown Risks

Substantial. Broad equity indices have historically experienced declines exceeding half their value in severe episodes, with recovery periods that have at times extended over many years. Individual sectors, countries and styles have experienced deeper and longer drawdowns.

### Correlation Behaviour

Global equity markets share a large common factor, so geographic diversification within equities reduces but does not eliminate common exposure. Correlations across regions, sectors and styles have tended to rise during stress. The correlation with nominal government bonds has been notably unstable and regime-dependent — see §4.2.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Participation in productive corporate growth and long-horizon real return; a primary source of long-term growth exposure; an income source through dividends.

### Main Risks

Earnings disappointment; valuation compression; discount-rate shocks; concentration in a few dominant companies within capitalisation-weighted indices; sector and geographic concentration; currency risk on unhedged foreign holdings; sequence-of-returns risk for investors drawing on capital.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Prolonged periods of high inflation with rising real rates, where nominal earnings growth fails to offset multiple compression; extended stagnation with weak nominal growth; environments where index concentration means broad equity exposure is effectively a small number of large bets; and periods where the recovery horizon exceeds the investor's actual horizon, converting a temporary decline into a realised permanent loss.

## 4.2 Government Bonds (nominal)

### Primary Return Drivers

Coupon income; changes in nominal yields, decomposable into changes in real yields, inflation expectations and the term premium; the issuer's credit standing; and currency for foreign-denominated holdings.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Growth disappointments and recessions accompanied by falling inflation; flight-to-quality episodes for the most creditworthy issuers; disinflationary periods; environments of monetary easing.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Inflation surprises to the upside; rapid rises in policy rates or term premia; deteriorating fiscal credibility for the issuer; environments where the dominant shock is inflationary rather than growth-driven.

### Growth Sensitivity

Generally negative for high-quality issuers — weaker growth has tended to be associated with lower yields and higher prices. This inverse relationship is the mechanism behind the diversifying role, and it is conditional on the growth weakness not being accompanied by inflation.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Strongly negative. The cash flows are fixed in nominal terms, so unexpected inflation directly erodes their real value and typically raises yields. **This is the single most important vulnerability of the asset class and the primary condition under which its diversifying role fails.**

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

The defining characteristic, determined by duration. Longer-dated bonds are substantially more price-sensitive to yield changes than shorter-dated ones. Convexity means this sensitivity is itself not constant. Duration should always be stated; "bonds" without a duration figure is an underspecified exposure.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Major sovereign bond markets are typically among the deepest and most liquid available. Smaller sovereign markets, longer maturities and periods of stress can see materially reduced liquidity, and even major markets have experienced episodic dysfunction.

### Volatility Characteristics

Volatility scales approximately with duration. Short-dated high-quality government bonds have historically exhibited low volatility; long-dated bonds have at times exhibited volatility comparable to equities, particularly during rate-regime shifts.

### Drawdown Risks

Frequently underestimated. Long-duration bonds can experience large drawdowns in real terms during periods of rising yields, and the recovery mechanism differs from equities: a bond held to maturity returns par in nominal terms, but the purchasing power lost to inflation is not recovered. **Nominal safety is not real safety.**

### Correlation Behaviour

The most important and most regime-dependent correlation in portfolio construction. Over some extended periods the equity–bond correlation has been negative, with bonds cushioning equity declines. Over others it has been positive, with both falling together. The distinguishing condition has generally been the inflation regime: negative correlation has been more common when inflation was low and stable and shocks were growth-driven; positive correlation has been more common when inflation was high or volatile and shocks were inflation- or rate-driven. **Any portfolio that depends on a negative equity–bond correlation is depending on a regime, not on a property.**

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Contractual income; potential cushioning of growth-driven equity drawdowns; duration exposure as a deliberate position; a liability-matching instrument where future obligations are known and nominal; a deflation-scenario exposure.

### Main Risks

Inflation risk; duration risk; sovereign credit risk (which varies enormously across issuers and is not negligible for all); reinvestment risk; currency risk on unhedged foreign holdings; fiscal-credibility risk where debt issuance is large relative to demand.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Inflation shocks, where bonds fall alongside equities and provide no cushion at precisely the moment the cushion is expected. Rapid tightening cycles. Fiscal-credibility events, where the "risk-free" characterisation ceases to apply. Environments where real yields rise sharply from low starting levels. And the structural case: when the starting yield is low, the buffer available from falling yields is mechanically limited, reducing the asset's capacity to cushion a subsequent shock.

## 4.3 Inflation-Linked Bonds

### Primary Return Drivers

Real yield changes; the inflation accrual applied to principal and coupons; the issuer's credit standing; liquidity conditions; and, for foreign holdings, currency and the fact that the linkage is to *that jurisdiction's* price index.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Periods when realised inflation exceeds what was priced at purchase; environments of rising inflation expectations with contained real rates; periods where inflation risk is under-compensated in market pricing.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Sharp rises in real yields, which can produce losses even when inflation is high; disinflationary periods where the inflation accrual is minimal; deflation, subject to any deflation floor in the instrument's terms; liquidity stress, where these markets have at times been less liquid than their nominal counterparts.

### Growth Sensitivity

Indirect, operating primarily through the effect of growth on real rates. Strong growth associated with rising real rates has tended to be unfavourable.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Positive by construction — this is the instrument's purpose. The bond's principal and coupons are contractually linked to a *specific published index*, so the investor receives the realised inflation captured by that index, subject to an indexation lag and to any mismatch between the index and the investor's personal cost of living. This contractual exposure to realised inflation should be distinguished from the instrument's *relative* performance against nominal bonds: that relative outcome depends importantly on how realised inflation compares with the inflation expectations, or breakeven inflation, already embedded in the bond's price at purchase. **An inflation-linked bond bought when breakeven inflation is already high can still underperform a comparable nominal bond if realised inflation comes in below that embedded expectation, even though the linked bond still delivered its contractual inflation-linked return.**

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Sensitive to *real* rates rather than nominal rates. This is the key structural distinction from nominal bonds: it separates real-rate exposure from inflation exposure, which nominal bonds bundle together. Duration still governs the magnitude of the real-rate sensitivity.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Generally less liquid than the equivalent nominal sovereign market. Liquidity varies substantially across issuers and has at times deteriorated sharply in stress, occasionally causing prices to move in ways not explained by real yields or inflation.

### Volatility Characteristics

Driven by real-yield volatility and duration. Can be meaningfully volatile, particularly at longer maturities, and are not a low-volatility asset simply because they are indexed.

### Drawdown Risks

Real, and often surprising to holders who expected inflation linkage to prevent losses. Rising real yields can produce substantial declines during periods of high inflation — the combination that most confounds expectations.

### Correlation Behaviour

Tends to correlate with nominal bonds through the shared real-rate component, while diverging when inflation expectations move. Correlation with equities is regime-dependent. Provides more reliable differentiation from nominal bonds specifically in inflationary environments.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Explicit exposure to the inflationary environments where nominal bonds are most vulnerable; a real-return anchor for long-dated real liabilities; a means of holding duration without full inflation exposure.

### Main Risks

Real-rate risk; the mismatch between the reference index and the investor's actual cost of living; indexation lag; liquidity risk; issuer credit risk; tax treatment of the inflation accrual, which in some jurisdictions is taxed before it is received; and the risk of paying an elevated inflation premium at purchase.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Rising real yields during inflationary periods, producing losses despite the inflation linkage. Purchase at breakevens that already embed high expected inflation, which limits the bond's capacity to outperform nominal bonds even if inflation subsequently proves elevated. Liquidity dislocations. A divergence between the reference index and the investor's actual expenditure basket. And the general case that the contractual link to realised inflation does not by itself guarantee favourable *relative* performance against nominal bonds — that depends importantly on how realised inflation compares with the breakeven inflation already priced in at purchase.

## 4.4 Cash and Short-Duration Instruments

### Primary Return Drivers

Prevailing short-term interest rates; the credit quality of the deposit-taker or issuer; and, for foreign holdings, currency movements.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Periods of rising policy rates, when longer-duration assets reprice downward and short instruments reset quickly to higher rates; risk-off episodes where most assets decline; environments where positive real short rates are available; periods of general market dislocation, where the option value of holding deployable capital is high.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Periods of negative real rates, where purchasing power erodes steadily; strong bull markets in risk assets, where the opportunity cost is large; falling-rate environments, which introduce reinvestment risk.

### Growth Sensitivity

Low direct sensitivity, though policy rates respond to growth conditions with a lag, giving an indirect and delayed link.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Ambiguous and frequently misunderstood. Rates typically rise in response to inflation, providing partial and lagged compensation. But during inflation surges, real returns on cash have frequently been negative for extended periods. **Cash is not an inflation hedge; it is an instrument whose compensation for inflation arrives late and incompletely.**

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Minimal price sensitivity by construction — the defining characteristic. The exposure is to reinvestment rather than to price: when rates fall, the income falls, and there is no offsetting capital gain.

### Liquidity Characteristics

The highest of any asset class, and the reference point against which other assets' liquidity is measured. Some instruments carry notice periods, penalties or gating provisions that qualify this.

### Volatility Characteristics

Very low nominal volatility. Real volatility is not zero and depends on inflation. Foreign-currency cash carries the full volatility of the currency pair, which can be substantial.

### Drawdown Risks

Nominal drawdown is minimal for high-quality instruments. **Real drawdown can be significant and cumulative**: extended periods of negative real rates produce a steady, compounding loss of purchasing power that is easy to overlook precisely because the nominal balance never falls.

### Correlation Behaviour

Low correlation with most risk assets, which makes cash a genuine diversifier in the narrow sense. It does not, however, appreciate when other assets fall — it merely fails to decline. This is a meaningful distinction from an asset that provides a positive offset.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Liquidity reserve to meet spending needs and obligations without forced selling; a buffer that prevents drawdowns in other assets from becoming realised losses; dry powder for rebalancing into declines; a natural holding for short-horizon liabilities; a volatility dampener.

### Main Risks

Purchasing-power erosion; reinvestment risk; deposit-taker or counterparty credit risk beyond any applicable protection scheme limits; currency risk on foreign holdings; and the behavioural risk of over-holding cash and remaining out of markets indefinitely.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Sustained negative real rates, where the "safe" asset produces reliable real losses. High-inflation environments. Counterparty failure where holdings exceed protection limits. Instruments whose liquidity is less than assumed under stress. And the extended-underperformance case, where cash held for safety over long periods materially impairs the achievement of long-horizon objectives.

`CONFIGURABLE PARAMETER:` minimum liquidity reserve, expressed relative to the investor's obligations and spending needs. *No value is chosen here.*

## 4.5 Gold

### Primary Return Drivers

Real interest rates (a widely observed inverse relationship, though not a stable one); currency movements, particularly of the currency in which gold is priced; monetary policy credibility and debasement concerns; central-bank and official-sector demand; investment and speculative demand; jewellery and industrial demand; and geopolitical risk premia.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Periods of falling or negative real interest rates; environments where confidence in monetary or fiscal management is questioned; currency debasement concerns; certain geopolitical crises; periods of sustained official-sector accumulation.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Rising real interest rates, which raise the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset; periods of strong confidence in monetary institutions; strength in the pricing currency; environments where competing real-return assets offer attractive yields.

### Growth Sensitivity

Low and inconsistent. Gold is not a claim on productive activity and has behaved as both a crisis asset and a risk-on asset in different periods.

### Inflation Sensitivity

**Frequently overstated and inconsistent.** Gold has performed strongly during some inflationary episodes and poorly during others, including some periods of elevated inflation. The relationship with *real interest rates* has generally been more consistent than the relationship with realised inflation. Describing gold as an inflation hedge without this qualification is one of the more common errors in portfolio discussion, and EducosysDalio should not do it.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Generally negative with respect to real rates. Because gold produces no income, its relative attractiveness declines when real yields on income-producing assets rise. This is the more reliable of gold's observed relationships, though "more reliable" is not "stable."

### Liquidity Characteristics

Physical gold and major gold-backed exchange-traded instruments are generally liquid. Physical holding introduces storage, insurance, authentication and dealing-spread considerations. The form in which gold is held matters materially to its practical liquidity.

### Volatility Characteristics

Historically comparable to equity-level volatility over many periods, with extended trending behaviour in both directions. It is not a low-volatility asset.

### Drawdown Risks

Substantial and potentially very prolonged. Gold has experienced multi-year and, in some historical periods, multi-decade declines in real terms. Because it generates no income, there is no cash-flow return during a drawdown to offset the wait.

### Correlation Behaviour

Historically low average correlation with equities and bonds, which is the basis for its diversification case. The correlation is unstable and has at times turned positive, including during some liquidity events where gold was sold to meet obligations elsewhere. Its behaviour tends to be most distinctive during monetary and currency stress specifically.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

A monetary and real-asset diversifier with return drivers substantially different from productive assets; an exposure to scenarios involving monetary-system stress, currency debasement or falling real rates; a store of value with no counterparty or credit dependence when held in appropriate form.

### Main Risks

No contractual cash flows and no intrinsic yield — the return depends entirely on price appreciation; high volatility; potentially very long drawdowns; storage, insurance and custody costs; currency exposure; the risk that any of its historical relationships fails to hold; and, for some holding structures, counterparty exposure.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Sustained rises in real interest rates. Liquidity crises where gold is sold for funding rather than bought for safety. Extended periods of monetary credibility and positive real yields. Purchase after the debasement narrative is already widely held and priced. And the general case that gold's role rests on demand for a monetary alternative — a demand that is behavioural and can shift without warning.

## 4.6 Broad Commodities

### Primary Return Drivers

For futures-based exposure, returns decompose into three distinct components that are frequently conflated: the change in the **spot** price; the **roll** yield arising from the shape of the futures curve; and the **collateral** return earned on the cash backing the position. The roll component can dominate over extended periods, meaning realised returns can differ substantially from spot price changes.

Underlying spot drivers include physical supply and demand, inventory levels, production costs, geopolitical supply disruption, weather, currency movements, and industrial demand.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Supply-driven inflation shocks; periods of strong global industrial demand; environments where supply is constrained relative to demand; backwardated curve conditions that generate positive roll; some geopolitical disruptions.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Demand collapses and recessions; disinflationary environments; periods of supply abundance following capacity expansion; sustained contango, which generates negative roll and can erode returns even when spot prices are stable or rising modestly.

### Growth Sensitivity

Generally positive for industrially-linked commodities, since demand is tied to activity. Energy and agricultural commodities can be dominated by supply factors that override the demand relationship entirely — a supply disruption can drive prices up during weak growth.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Among the more positive of the major asset classes, particularly for supply-driven and energy-led inflation, since commodities are inputs to the price indices themselves. The relationship is stronger for unexpected inflation than for expected inflation. Demand-driven inflation and supply-driven inflation produce different commodity outcomes, and the distinction matters.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Indirect. Higher rates raise the cost of carrying inventory and can strengthen the pricing currency, both generally unfavourable. The collateral return component benefits from higher short rates, partially offsetting this.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Major futures markets are generally liquid. Individual commodities vary widely. Physical commodity holding is impractical for most investors, so exposure is typically obtained through futures, funds or equities — each with different characteristics and different tracking behaviour relative to spot.

### Volatility Characteristics

Historically high, with individual commodities frequently more volatile than broad equity indices. Broad baskets are less volatile than single commodities but remain a high-volatility exposure.

### Drawdown Risks

Substantial and prolonged. Commodity markets have experienced extended bear periods lasting many years, and the absence of income means no cash-flow return during those periods.

### Correlation Behaviour

Historically low to moderate average correlation with equities and bonds, with the important property that the correlation has tended to be *most favourable* in inflationary environments — where nominal bonds are most vulnerable. Correlation with equities has risen during global demand shocks, when both decline together.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Exposure to supply-driven inflation, which is one of the environments most damaging to conventional stock-and-bond portfolios; diversification of return drivers away from corporate cash flows and discount rates; a distinct source of environmental coverage.

### Main Risks

No income generation; roll cost in contango markets; high volatility; long drawdowns; the tracking difference between the chosen exposure vehicle and the underlying commodities; counterparty and structural risk in some products; storage and financing economics embedded in futures pricing; and the fact that commodity equities behave partly as equities rather than as commodities.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Demand-driven recessions where commodities fall alongside equities, providing no diversification when it is most needed. Sustained contango eroding returns despite stable spot prices. Disinflationary environments producing prolonged underperformance. Supply responses that reverse price gains. And exposure vehicles that fail to deliver the intended economic exposure.

## 4.7 Property / REITs

### Primary Return Drivers

Rental income and its growth; property valuations, driven by capitalisation rates and therefore by interest rates and risk premia; occupancy and tenant credit quality; the leverage employed within the structure; development and transaction activity; and, for listed vehicles, equity market sentiment.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Periods of moderate inflation with rising rents and stable or falling capitalisation rates; falling real interest rates; strong occupier demand; environments where lease structures allow rents to reset upward with inflation.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Rapidly rising interest rates, which raise capitalisation rates and financing costs simultaneously; credit contractions, which restrict the debt on which the sector depends; recessions that impair occupancy and tenant solvency; structural demand shifts affecting particular property types; oversupply following development booms.

### Growth Sensitivity

Positive, transmitted through occupancy, rental growth and tenant health. Sensitivity varies substantially by property type — some segments are more defensive, others closely tied to discretionary activity.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Mixed and dependent on lease structure. Where leases contain inflation-linked or regularly resetting rents, property can provide partial inflation protection. Where leases are long and fixed, it may not. Rising inflation accompanied by rising rates has frequently been unfavourable, because the valuation effect of higher capitalisation rates can outweigh the rental benefit.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

High, and operating through two reinforcing channels: capitalisation rates typically rise with rates, reducing valuations; and financing costs rise, reducing net income for leveraged structures. This dual sensitivity is why property is often more rate-sensitive than a simple income-asset framing suggests.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Sharply divided by vehicle. Listed REITs trade with equity-like liquidity. Direct property is highly illiquid with long transaction times and high costs. Open-ended unlisted property funds carry a structural liquidity mismatch between their redemption terms and their underlying assets, and have at times suspended redemptions during stress — precisely when access was most wanted.

### Volatility Characteristics

Listed REITs have historically exhibited equity-like volatility. Direct and appraisal-valued property shows much lower *measured* volatility, but this is substantially an artefact of infrequent, smoothed valuation rather than a reflection of lower economic risk. **Measured volatility for appraisal-valued property should be treated as understated.**

### Drawdown Risks

Substantial. Listed REITs have experienced severe drawdowns in credit and rate shocks, amplified by structural leverage. Direct property has experienced large declines that appeared gradually in valuations while transaction markets were effectively frozen.

### Correlation Behaviour

Listed REITs correlate meaningfully with equities, particularly in stress, which limits their diversification value in exactly the conditions where it is sought. Direct property shows lower measured correlation, largely due to valuation smoothing rather than genuine independence. Sensitivity to rates creates a further link to bonds.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Income generation with some inflation linkage where lease structures permit; exposure to a real asset with cash flows; a return driver partly distinct from corporate earnings; potential portfolio diversification where the exposure genuinely differs from existing equity holdings.

### Main Risks

Interest-rate risk through the dual channel; embedded leverage; tenant credit and occupancy risk; illiquidity and redemption gating in unlisted vehicles; structural obsolescence of particular property types; concentration risk, especially where the investor already owns a home in the same market; and the tendency for measured risk statistics to understate true risk.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Rapidly rising rates, where both valuation and financing deteriorate together. Credit contractions restricting refinancing. Recessions impairing occupancy. Structural demand shifts rendering existing space obsolete. Liquidity gating preventing exit. And the specific case where a listed REIT allocation held as a diversifier behaves as a leveraged, rate-sensitive equity position during stress.

**Note on the investor's own home.** For many investors, residential property is already the dominant asset on the household balance sheet, often financed with substantial leverage. Additional property exposure should be assessed against that existing concentration rather than in isolation.

## 4.8 Foreign Currency Exposure

### Primary Return Drivers

Interest rate differentials between currencies; relative inflation rates; the current account and trade position; capital flows; relative monetary and fiscal policy; terms of trade; risk sentiment, which affects safe-haven and funding currencies distinctively; and, over long horizons, relative purchasing power.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

For a given foreign currency, environments favouring that currency's economy or offering rising relative rates. For safe-haven currencies specifically, risk-off episodes. For commodity-linked currencies, commodity strength.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Environments unfavourable to that currency's economy; falling relative rates; deteriorating fiscal or monetary credibility; risk-off episodes for higher-yielding and emerging-market currencies; and, for the investor, any period in which their home currency strengthens broadly.

### Growth Sensitivity

Indirect and relative. What matters is the growth *differential* between economies and how it affects expected policy paths, not the absolute growth of either.

### Inflation Sensitivity

Relative. Higher inflation in one economy than another has tended, over long horizons, to be associated with depreciation of that currency, though the relationship is weak and slow over shorter periods and frequently dominated by rate differentials and flows.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

High in the short to medium term. Rate differentials and changes in expected policy paths are among the most immediate drivers of currency movements.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Major currency pairs are among the most liquid markets globally. Emerging-market currencies vary widely and can become sharply illiquid in stress, sometimes with capital controls or trading restrictions imposed at the worst moment.

### Volatility Characteristics

Major-pair volatility is typically lower than equity volatility but is far from trivial and can be substantial over the medium term. Emerging-market currency volatility can be very high, with occasional discontinuous moves.

### Drawdown Risks

Currency movements can materially erode the returns of an otherwise successful foreign investment, or amplify losses. Over multi-year horizons, currency effects have at times been comparable in magnitude to the underlying asset returns. Emerging-market currencies have experienced severe and sometimes abrupt depreciations.

### Correlation Behaviour

Highly variable across pairs and regimes. Some currencies have historically strengthened during risk-off episodes and others weakened, producing correlations that can be helpful or harmful depending on which currency is held. Correlations shift with the prevailing funding and safe-haven dynamics.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Diversification of purchasing-power risk away from a single currency and a single set of domestic policy outcomes; access to foreign assets whose exposure is inseparable from currency exposure; a hedge against domestic monetary or fiscal deterioration.

### Main Risks

Unrewarded volatility, since currency exposure does not carry a clear long-run expected return premium in the way equity risk does; the cost and complexity of hedging; the mismatch between the currency of assets and the currency of liabilities; capital controls and convertibility risk in some markets; and, most fundamentally, the risk of holding assets denominated in currencies the investor will never spend.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Sustained appreciation of the home currency, eroding foreign returns over long periods. Correlation shifts that cause currency losses to coincide with asset losses. Emerging-market currency crises. Hedging costs exceeding the risk-reduction benefit. And the case where an unhedged foreign allocation held for diversification instead introduces a large, unrewarded and unmanaged additional risk.

**Framing note.** Currency exposure is best treated as a separable decision from the underlying asset. Holding a foreign equity fund is two positions: the equities and the currency. They can, in principle, be managed independently, and EducosysDalio should present them as distinct exposures.

`CONFIGURABLE PARAMETER:` the target proportion of foreign currency exposure to be hedged, if any. *No value is chosen here.*

## 4.9 Bitcoin / Crypto

**EducosysDalio does not assign this asset class a settled portfolio role.** It is not classified as digital gold, an inflation hedge, a safe haven, or an uncorrelated asset. Each of those characterisations is a claim that requires evidence across a range of environments, and the available history is short relative to the range of environments a long-horizon portfolio must survive. The role must remain evidence-driven and provisional.

### Primary Return Drivers

Speculative demand and flows; adoption and network usage; narrative shifts, including monetary-debasement and store-of-value framings; global liquidity conditions, to which crypto has at times shown high sensitivity; regulatory developments; market structure, including the behaviour of exchanges, custodians and leveraged participants; and, for some assets, protocol-specific technological and governance factors.

### Typical Environments of Relative Strength

Periods of abundant liquidity and strong risk appetite; phases of accelerating adoption or favourable regulatory clarity; episodes where monetary-debasement narratives gain traction; speculative momentum phases.

### Typical Environments of Weakness

Liquidity tightening and rising real rates; risk-off episodes; adverse regulatory developments; failures of major exchanges, custodians or lending intermediaries; deleveraging within the crypto ecosystem itself; periods of narrative reversal.

### Growth Sensitivity

Not well established. Observed behaviour has at times resembled that of a long-duration, high-beta risk asset — rising with risk appetite and falling with it — rather than tracking economic growth directly.

### Inflation Sensitivity

**Not established, and frequently misrepresented.** The fixed-supply argument advanced for Bitcoin is a claim about issuance schedules, not a demonstrated empirical relationship with realised inflation. Observed behaviour during inflationary periods has been inconsistent, and in some such periods the asset declined substantially. EducosysDalio should not describe crypto as an inflation hedge.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Observed behaviour has often been consistent with high sensitivity to real rates and liquidity conditions — declining as rates rose and liquidity tightened. This is more characteristic of a speculative long-duration risk asset than of a monetary safe haven. The sample supporting this is short.

### Liquidity Characteristics

Highly variable. Major assets on major venues can be liquid in normal conditions. Liquidity has deteriorated sharply during stress, and has at times disappeared entirely at the venue level due to exchange failure, withdrawal suspension or operational breakdown. Access to an asset and ownership of an asset are not the same thing in this market. Smaller tokens can be extremely illiquid.

### Volatility Characteristics

Very high — historically a multiple of broad equity volatility, with frequent large single-day moves. Volatility varies over time but has remained elevated relative to traditional asset classes throughout the available history.

### Drawdown Risks

Severe. Major crypto assets have experienced repeated, extremely severe drawdowns, with extended recovery periods. Many smaller tokens have declined to near-zero and not recovered. Total loss is a realistic outcome for individual assets in this category, not a tail scenario.

### Correlation Behaviour

**Unstable and not reliably low.** Correlation with equities and other risk assets has varied considerably and has at times been substantially positive, including during risk-off episodes when diversification was most needed. The early-period low correlation that grounded much of the diversification argument was measured over a short window during a period of limited institutional participation, and should not be assumed to persist.

### Potential Role in a Diversified Portfolio

Genuinely uncertain. Arguments advanced include exposure to a distinct adoption-driven return stream, a hedge against monetary-system disruption, and access to a new asset category. None of these is established by the available evidence. EducosysDalio's position is that any role is provisional and must be justified by ongoing evidence rather than by narrative, and that position sizing should reflect the possibility of total loss.

### Main Risks

Extreme volatility; severe and potentially permanent drawdowns; regulatory risk, including the possibility of restriction or prohibition in particular jurisdictions; custody risk, including exchange failure, hacking, fraud and irrecoverable loss of access; technological and protocol risk; market-structure risk, including thin order books, high embedded leverage and cascading liquidations; concentration of ownership; the absence of cash flows or any conventional valuation anchor; tax and reporting complexity; and the risk that the currently believed portfolio role is simply wrong.

### Conditions Under Which the Expected Role Can Break Down

Essentially all of the proposed roles are conditional and have already failed at times within the short available history:

- The **inflation hedge** role has broken down in observed inflationary periods.
- The **safe haven** role has broken down in risk-off episodes where crypto fell alongside equities.
- The **uncorrelated diversifier** role has broken down as correlation with risk assets rose.
- The **digital gold** role rests on a monetary-adoption thesis that remains unproven over a full cycle.
- The **store of value** role: the magnitude and frequency of historical drawdowns materially weaken Bitcoin's suitability as a short- or medium-horizon capital-preservation asset, even if a longer-term monetary store-of-value thesis remains under debate and is not settled purely by drawdown history.

Additional breakdown conditions include regulatory prohibition, custody failure, technological compromise, and liquidity evaporation during stress.

**Handling requirement.** Where an investor holds crypto, EducosysDalio should: quantify the position's risk contribution rather than its capital weight (which will substantially understate it); state the drawdown history explicitly; identify what role the investor believes it plays and test that belief against evidence; assess custody and access arrangements as a distinct risk; and confirm that the position is sized such that a total loss would not compromise the investor's objectives. EducosysDalio should neither dismiss the asset class nor endorse it.

`CONFIGURABLE PARAMETER:` maximum acceptable risk contribution from assets with limited history or unestablished portfolio role. *No value is chosen here.*

## 5. Portfolio Implications

Applying this file to a real portfolio should produce:

**Role verification.** For each holding, what role does the investor believe it plays, and does the evidence support that belief in the environments that matter? Mismatches between believed role and actual behaviour are among the most valuable findings the system can surface.

**Breakdown-condition mapping.** For each holding, under what conditions would its expected role fail — and do several holdings share the same breakdown condition? A portfolio in which three different assets all fail under the same inflation shock is less diversified than its composition suggests.

**Sub-category specification.** "Bonds" is insufficient. Duration, credit quality, currency and issuer must be recorded, because within-category variation frequently exceeds between-category variation.

**Environmental coverage.** Which environments have positive exposure and which have none, using the framing in `all_weather.md`.

**Measurement caveats.** Which holdings have measured risk statistics that understate true risk — appraisal-valued property, illiquid vehicles, short-history assets. These require explicit flagging so that `risk_balancing.md` outputs are not read at face value.

**Household context.** Existing property, employment income, business ownership and personal leverage are exposures too, and portfolio assets should be assessed against them rather than in isolation. An investor whose income depends on one sector and whose portfolio concentrates in that sector holds one large bet, not two positions.

## 6. EducosysDalio Interpretation

**6.1 Conditional language always.** Roles are stated as tendencies with conditions, never as properties. The formulation is "has tended to, under conditions X" rather than "is."

**6.2 Breakdown conditions are mandatory.** No role may be described without its breakdown conditions. A role described without them is an incomplete and potentially misleading statement.

**6.3 No ranking, no percentages.** EducosysDalio does not rank assets as good or bad, and does not produce model allocations. Suitability depends on the investor and the portfolio context.

**6.4 Sub-category detail is preserved.** Holdings are analysed with their actual characteristics, not collapsed into category labels.

**6.5 The word "safe" is avoided.** Every asset trades one risk for another. What is safe depends on the liability being matched.

**6.6 Crypto is treated as unresolved.** Neither dismissed nor endorsed; described accurately, sized with reference to the possibility of total loss, and reassessed as evidence accumulates.

**6.7 Belief testing.** Where an investor states a rationale for holding something, EducosysDalio tests that rationale against the asset's actual behaviour and breakdown conditions. Correcting a mistaken belief about *why* something is held is often more valuable than changing the holding.

**6.8 The household balance sheet is in scope.** Property, human capital, business interests and personal debt are exposures.

**6.9 Shared breakdown conditions are flagged.** When multiple holdings fail under the same condition, that is a concentration finding regardless of how different the assets appear.

## 7. Failure Modes / Misinterpretations

**Shorthand roles.** Reducing assets to slogans — the specific failure this file exists to prevent.

**Treating roles as unconditional.** Assuming government bonds will cushion any equity decline, or that gold will rise with any inflation.

**Ignoring within-class variation.** Treating all bonds, all equities or all property as homogeneous.

**Accepting understated risk statistics.** Reading measured volatility for appraisal-valued or short-history assets at face value.

**Extrapolating short histories.** Drawing confident conclusions about assets whose available record covers few environments — most acutely relevant to crypto, but also to newer fund structures and strategies.

**Narrative-based role assignment.** Assigning a role because a story is compelling rather than because behaviour supports it.

**Assuming stable correlations.** Particularly the equity–bond relationship, whose instability has material portfolio consequences.

**Overlooking currency as a separate exposure.** Treating a foreign holding as a single position.

**Ignoring the vehicle.** The same economic exposure held through different structures — physical, futures, fund, listed vehicle — can behave very differently in liquidity and stress terms.

**Analysing the portfolio in isolation from the household.**

**Presenting nominal safety as real safety.** Cash and nominal bonds carry purchasing-power risk that does not appear in nominal volatility statistics.

**Treating this file as a shopping list.** It describes characteristics; it does not recommend holdings.

## 8. Questions EducosysDalio Should Ask

**About each holding**

- What role does the investor believe this plays, and does its behaviour support that belief?
- What are its primary return drivers, and do they overlap with other holdings?
- Under what specific conditions would its expected role break down?
- What are its actual sub-category characteristics — duration, credit quality, currency, geography, style, vehicle?
- Is its measured volatility likely to understate true risk?
- How long is the available history, and how many distinct environments does it cover?

**About the portfolio**

- Do multiple holdings share a common breakdown condition?
- Which environments have no positive exposure at all?
- Which holdings are relied upon to perform in stress, and what is the evidence that they will?
- Is any holding's diversification case based on a correlation measured over a short or unrepresentative window?
- Does the portfolio depend on the equity–bond correlation remaining negative?

**About the household**

- What does the investor already own outside the portfolio — property, business interests, concentrated employment exposure?
- What debt does the investor carry, and on what terms?
- Does the portfolio compound an existing household concentration or offset it?

**About specific vulnerabilities**

- In which currency does the investor spend, and how much exposure sits elsewhere?
- Which holdings could not be sold quickly if needed, and could that constraint bind at a bad time?
- For any holding with unestablished role, is the position sized such that total loss would not compromise objectives?

**The governing question**

- If the environment turns out to be different from the one currently assumed, which of these assets would fail to perform its expected role — and would the portfolio still be viable?

## 9. Structured Knowledge Summary

**Principle:**
Asset roles are conditional consequences of environmental sensitivities, not intrinsic properties; every role has conditions under which it breaks down, and those conditions matter more than the role itself.

**Objective:**
Give EducosysDalio a structured, nuanced characterisation of each asset class's drivers, sensitivities, risks and breakdown conditions, sufficient to test whether a portfolio's holdings can perform the roles the investor believes they perform.

**Relevant Inputs:**
Holdings with full sub-category detail (duration, credit quality, currency, geography, style, vehicle structure); return histories with coverage and quality flags; the investor's stated rationale for each holding; household balance-sheet context including property, human capital and leverage; liquidity and dealing terms; environmental assessment from `economic_regimes.md`.

**Relevant Outputs:**
Per-holding role assessment with breakdown conditions; identification of mismatches between believed and evidenced roles; shared breakdown-condition findings; environmental coverage map; measurement-caveat flags for holdings with understated risk statistics; sub-category exposure detail; household-context concentration findings.

**Risks Addressed:**
Misplaced reliance on assumed asset roles; regime-dependent role failure; correlation instability, particularly equity–bond; hidden within-class variation; understated risk in illiquid and short-history assets; narrative-driven position holding; household concentration; unresolved-role assets sized as though their role were established.

**Agent Behaviours:**
Use conditional, hedged language exclusively; always state breakdown conditions; never rank assets or produce model allocations; preserve sub-category detail; avoid the word "safe"; treat crypto as unresolved and size it accordingly; test the investor's stated rationale against evidence; include the household balance sheet; flag shared breakdown conditions as concentration.

**Deterministic Calculations Required:**
Mapping of holdings to asset categories with look-through and sub-category attributes; per-holding volatility, drawdown and correlation statistics (computed per `risk_balancing.md`); conditional correlations within defined environmental periods; historical performance of holdings within defined regime periods where data permits; data coverage and history-length flags.

**Configurable Parameters:**
Asset category and sub-category taxonomy; minimum history length required before a holding's statistics are treated as meaningful; foreign currency hedge target; maximum acceptable risk contribution from assets with limited history or unestablished role; minimum liquidity reserve. *No values are set in this file.*

**Related Knowledge Files:**
`investment_philosophy.md`, `all_weather.md`, `diversification.md`, `risk_balancing.md`, `economic_regimes.md`, `debt_cycles.md`.
